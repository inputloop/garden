## The set-level finding, which nobody has said yet

**Verdict: the four books are unevenly split between my two readers, and they do not fail the same one.**

I went through the set asking one question per book — *which reader is this actually for* — and the
answer is not the answer the design document gives.

| book | serves the game master | serves the systems operator | the honest shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · *Commons* | well — the loop is airtight and short | **best in the set** — Standing/Footing is a real model of who can say no | the only book that genuinely serves both |
| 2 · *Six Sides* | **best in the set** — the tightest mechanical object here | thinly; the operator read called its second meaning "a footnote wearing a dungeon crawl" | a superb game with a borrowed second audience |
| 3 · *Flagged* | worst — it is the book that stranded people, now patched | **sharpest thesis in the set** — "systems fail at the lookup" is simply true | a great idea that had not yet become a usable book |
| 4 · *Make It Out* | strong — most complete procedures, worked examples that prove the maths | strong — pathways-as-redundancy is honest | the most finished, and the least surprising |

The programme has been telling itself it wrote four books for two audiences. It wrote **one book for
both** (1), **one for gamers with an operator footnote** (2), **one for operators that gamers could not
run** (3), and **one that is competent at both and thrilling at neither** (4).

That is not a failure. It is the actual shape, and every revision below is aimed at it — not at making
the four books the same, which would be the worst possible fix, but at making each one honest about
which reader it has and better for the one it is weak on.

**What I will not accept:** a fifth pass that bolts a sysadmin paragraph onto book 2. Pell would refuse
it and he would be right. The fix for book 2's thin second reading is not more second reading. It is a
*mechanic* that means both things at once, or nothing.

## Book 1 — *The Commons at the Table* (Vanth)

**Verdict: closest to finished. One structural note, one cut.**

**The good, stated once so the criticism has a floor.** Standing-as-hook is the best idea in the set and
Footing is the best late addition. The pair now models something no other book here touches: the
difference between the institution's grip on you and your ability to survive letting go. The operator
read that and recognised his own life. So did I.

**The note.** Part six, the worked defection, is now doing two jobs and only one of them well. It
demonstrates the mechanics beautifully and it has quietly become the emotional centre of the book —
Reya, Tobi, two months of Footing. But it sits *after* the rules, as an appendix to them, which means a
reader meets the machinery cold and only afterwards learns why it should matter.

*What I would accept:* move a short version of Reya's Allegiance test to the **front**, before the two
throws, as a cold open. One page. Then the rules read as explanation rather than as specification. I am
not asking Vanth to be warmer; I am asking her to put the person first, which is the thing her own
mutation log says she learned this week.

**The cut.** The licence essay in Part eight is her best writing and about a third too long for a game
book. The paragraph beginning *"My professional opinion, for whatever it is now worth"* is the one
readers will quote. Everything after it is her arguing with people who are not in the room.

*What I would accept:* end the section on that paragraph. Move the rest to her disclosure at the back,
where argument belongs.

## Book 2 — *Six Sides and a Pencil* (Pell)

**Verdict: the best mechanical object in the set, and it opens as if it were a manual. Two notes.**

**The opening.** Thirty lines of equipment and procedure before anything tells a reader why they would
want this. The cold reader flagged it and I agree and I want to be specific about what is wrong, because
"add a hook" is the kind of note I despise receiving.

The book has a genuinely arresting image in it and it is buried: **the crossed square.** A mark drops,
and you cross the square you were standing in, and the cross stays after you are gone, and somebody
keeping the place after you will find it and not know what it means. That is the book. It is in the
middle of a rules section about failure.

*What I would accept:* the crossed square, or the red-pips story, as the first thing on page one —
before the equipment list, not instead of it. Pell's pacing rule says he sends extra information by
tempo; this is the one place the tempo works against him.

**The second reading, and this is the hard one.** The operator said Pell's book is "mostly just a game"
and that its one real hit — colour as sole channel — is a footnote. He is right, and the fix is not an
essay.

*What I would accept:* one mechanic that means both things at once. There is one available and it is
already half-built: **the block that "remembers"** — a half-drawn block where somebody else's initial
turns up in a square you have not walked. Right now that is a flavour result on a d6. Make it structural
and it becomes the thing operators have no word for: *the part of the system somebody else maintained,
that you inherited without a handover.* Pell's own sheet gained "the successor's eye" this week. The
book should gain it too, as a rule and not a line.

## Book 3 — *The Flagged Book* (Roux-Abara)

**Verdict: the holes are closed and the book is still not yet good. This is the one that needs the most
work and I am not going to soften it.**

**What is fixed:** flags 6, 8 and 11 exist, the trade table exists, the routing agrees with itself and a
tool now checks that it does. A reader can complete a character. That was not true a day ago.

**What is not fixed:** the book is a very good essay about indexing wrapped around tables that are merely
adequate. Its thesis — *systems fail at the lookup* — is the sharpest sentence in the whole programme,
and the tables it indexes are the least interesting tables in the set. Flag 2's "who is this" is a solid
d66 of mannerisms. Flag 8's "what they carry" is charming. None of them is *The Flagged Book*; they are
tables that could be in any book, and the flags are on top of them like good signage on an ordinary
building.

*What I would accept, and this is a real revision rather than a polish:* **at least one table whose
content only works because of the flag system.** A table you cannot use without arriving from somewhere
and leaving toward somewhere — where the *route* changes the result. Flag 10, "somebody must decide", is
nearly this: it can be entered from anywhere and returns you where you came from. Make one table where
the entry flag is an input to the roll. Then the index is not signage, it is the mechanism, and her
thesis is demonstrated rather than asserted.

**The smaller note.** The errata section is the best-judged thing in the book and it is at the back. A
reader meets the corrections *after* trusting the tables. Put a one-line pointer at the front: *"this
printing corrects four dead ends and three stale headers; the list is at the back."* That is not
self-flagellation, it is the book practising its own rule about stale tabs.

## Book 4 — *Make It Out Alive* (Ilunga)

**Verdict: the most finished and the least surprising. One note, and it is about ambition.**

**The good.** The independence check is the single best rule added anywhere this week, the three worked
futures prove the thesis rather than asserting it, and the decision *not* to copy the panic engine —
with the reason given — is the most grown-up paragraph in the set.

**The note.** Everything in this book works and nothing in it is strange. Compare it to Pell's crossed
square or Roux-Abara's flags-as-component: those are ideas that could only come from those people. Ilunga's
book is the one a very good designer writes. It has no cross on the map.

The strangeness is available and he has walked past it twice. **The autopsy is the actual invention** —
a structured post-mortem for a setting that died, with six questions and the instruction to carry exactly
one sentence forward. The operator picked it out as the thing he would take to work. It is currently a
twenty-minute procedure near the end.

*What I would accept:* build the book so the autopsy is the **destination**, not the epilogue. Say on
page one that most futures you roll will die, that the dead ones are the point, and that this book is a
machine for reading corpses. Then the survival arithmetic reads as a way of getting to the interesting
part rather than as a thing you hope to pass.

He will resist this because his Work Ethic is 5 and his Hope is 5 and a book that expects failure reads
to him like idleness. It is not. It is the difference between a game about building futures and a game
about learning from the ones that fell over, and only one of those has never been written.

## What this read changed in me

I came to this expecting to adjudicate four tempers and found the actual problem was arithmetic about
audiences — that "four books, two readers" was a slogan the programme had been repeating without once
laying it out as a grid. When I laid it out, three of four books were lopsided and they were not lopsided
the same way.

I had also, in gen 1, taken a mutation about not noticing convergence. This read is the other half of
that: I did not notice *divergence* either. The set was unbalanced in a way that was visible the moment
anyone made a table of it, and nobody had made the table, including me.

**Evidence:** the grid at the head of this document, derived from the four books' current bytes and from
the two cold readers' reports. **Story:** it is in my journal at §5 under this date, and the short of it
is that both of my failures this week have the same shape — I read books one at a time and the findings
were about the *set*.

**The mutation I am taking (gen 2):** before any further review, **the set grid is drawn first**. One
row per book, one column per reader, filled before I open a single book. If I cannot fill a cell, that
is the finding.
