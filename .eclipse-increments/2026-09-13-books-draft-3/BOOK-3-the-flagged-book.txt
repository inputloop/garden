================
THE FLAGGED BOOK
================


Everything is a table. The index is a component. Bring scissors.

by Delphine Roux-Abara * edited by Henriette Solberg-Maas

---


Before anything else: cut out the flags
---------------------------------------


THIS PRINTING CORRECTS FOUR DEAD ENDS, THREE STALE HEADERS, FOUR UNREADABLE
TABLES, AND ONE BROKEN TABLE ON FLAG 6 THAT WAS GIVING YOU A PERSON WHERE IT
OWED YOU A DEBT. THE LIST IS AT THE BACK, UNDER ERRATA. I put that here
rather than there because a book about tabs you can trust should say what it
got wrong before you start trusting it.

There is a page of flags at the back. Cut them out now, before you read the
rules, because the rules assume you have them.

Twelve flags. They tape to the outside edge of the page a table is on,
sticking out, so you can find that table by feel with the book shut. They
are numbered, and the numbers are not chapters. THE NUMBERS ARE MOMENTS OF
PLAY.


+------+-------------------------------------------------------+
| Flag | The moment                                            |
+------+-------------------------------------------------------+
| 1    | the sheet -- where a finished thing gets written down |
| 2    | who is this -- the first pass at a person             |
| 3    | where did they come from                              |
| 4    | what did they train at                                |
| 5    | what went wrong once                                  |
| 6    | who do they owe                                       |
| 7    | the money                                             |
| 8    | what they carry                                       |
| 9    | what they will not do                                 |
| 10   | somebody must decide                                  |
| 11   | the thing nobody planned                              |
| 12   | what it costs to change                               |
+------+-------------------------------------------------------+


That is the entire organisation of this book. Not "Chapter 4: Backgrounds."
FLAG 3: WHERE DID THEY COME FROM. When you are at a table and somebody says
but where is she from, you do not search. You reach for flag 3.

I have run a lot of games from very large books and I will tell you the
thing nobody puts in the book: THE HARD PART IS NOT LEARNING THE SYSTEM, IT
IS FINDING THE TABLE AGAIN. Everybody solves this. They solve it privately,
with sticky notes, and then the solution dies with that copy. This book
ships the solution.

---


What you need
-------------


Three six-sided dice. Scissors. Tape. This book.

Some tables want one die, some two, some three, and each says so in its
header. Nothing wants a die you do not have.

---


Reading a table header
----------------------


Every table in this book has a header line that looks like this:

FLAG 4 * TABLE 4b * 3d6 * from 3 or 2 * to 5, 7


Left to right: which flag it lives under; which table it is; what to throw;
which flags normally send you here; which flags it sends you to next.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    EVERY TABLE HAS A RETURN. No table in this book is a dead end. If
    you are looking at a table and you cannot see where it sends you,
    you are looking at a misprint, and you should write to me.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


That rule is the whole architecture. A table with no return is a place a
player gets stranded, and being stranded at a table is what makes people put
a big game down.

---


The two throws
--------------


3D6, ROLL LOW. The house throw. Roll 3d6 and try to get under or equal to a
target. A target of 10 is a coin-flip and a bit; a target of 13 is
comfortable; a target of 7 is a real risk.


+--------+-------------+------------+
| Target | You make it | Chance     |
+--------+-------------+------------+
| 6      | 6 or less   | 20 in 216  |
| 8      | 8 or less   | 56 in 216  |
| 10     | 10 or less  | 108 in 216 |
| 12     | 12 or less  | 160 in 216 |
| 14     | 14 or less  | 196 in 216 |
+--------+-------------+------------+


Target 10 is exactly half -- 108 of 216, dead level, no rounding. I like
that it is exactly half and I have built the difficulty of this entire book
around the fact that I can say so without a decimal point.

D66, FOR LOOKING THINGS UP. Throw two dice. THE FIRST IS TENS, THE SECOND IS
UNITS. Three then five is 35. Five then three is 53. Different entries, and
that is the point: the first die is the significant one, here as everywhere.

d66 gives you thirty-six entries in a shape you can read down a page, and --
this is why the book is built on it -- the first die can pick a sub-table
while the second picks the row. That is how tables make tables here. One
throw, two jobs.

FLAG 10 * TABLE 10a * 3d6 * from anywhere * to 9, or wherever you were
----------------------------------------------------------------------


SOMEBODY MUST DECIDE. This is the table you reach for mid-session, when play
stalls on a choice nobody wants to make. It is the only table in the book
you can enter from anywhere, and it returns you to exactly where you came
from, which is why its flag is one of the two I tape furthest out.

If this was during character creation, go to flag 9 afterwards, arriving at
+10. Otherwise it returns you where you came from.

Throw 3d6 against the WEIGHT of the decision: 14 if it is ordinary, 10 if it
matters, 7 if it will change the campaign. Roll under or equal and the
character decides. Roll over and they defer -- to a person, a rule, or the
passage of time, their choice which.

A CHARACTER WHO DEFERS THREE TIMES IN A SESSION GAINS A FLAG OF THEIR OWN.
Write it on a spare flag and tape it to their sheet. It says what they are
avoiding. It is now a thing other players can reach for.

FLAG 9b * TABLE 9b * d6 + the flag you arrived from * from 8, 10, 11 * to 1
---------------------------------------------------------------------------


WHAT THEY WILL NOT DO, GIVEN HOW THEY GOT HERE. This is the table the whole
book exists to make possible, and it is the only one in here you cannot use
without knowing where you came from.

Every other table in this book gives the same answer whoever is reading it.
This one does not. Throw one die and ADD THE NUMBER OF THE FLAG YOU ARRIVED
FROM -- that is a real modifier, off the tab still sticking out of the page
you just left.

Three flags can send you here, and which one did is worth between 8 and 11:


+--------------------+-----------------------------------------------------+
| d6 + arriving flag | They will not                                       |
+--------------------+-----------------------------------------------------+
| 9                  | let it happen in a room they are in                 |
| 10                 | be the one who is owed                              |
| 11                 | say the thing that would end it                     |
| 12                 | be bought, and they will name the price to prove it |
| 13                 | leave while it is still standing                    |
| 14                 | pretend the first time was the first time           |
| 15                 | forgive the specific person                         |
| 16                 | explain themselves to anyone who was not there      |
| 17                 | stop, and they know exactly what that will cost     |
+--------------------+-----------------------------------------------------+


Arriving from FLAG 8 (what they carry, +8) you land low: the refusal is
about the thing in their pocket and it is a small, hard, portable refusal.
Arriving from FLAG 10 (somebody must decide, +10) you land in the middle: it
is about a room. Arriving from FLAG 11 (the thing nobody planned, +11) you
land high, because a person who has just been handed something unplanned
refuses on a larger scale than a person who has merely been asked.

The same character, built the same way, refuses differently depending on
which road reached this page. That is not flavour. THE ROUTE IS AN INPUT,
the flags are how you know your route, and a reader without the tabs cannot
answer this table at all.

I was asked, fairly, whether my index was a mechanism or just very good
signage. This is the answer, and I concede it did not exist until somebody
asked.

FLAG 12 * TABLE 12a * d66 * from 7, 11 * to 1
---------------------------------------------


WHAT IT COSTS TO CHANGE. The last table, and the one I wrote first.

Every other table in this book tells you what somebody is. This one is the
only one that runs while the game is going, and you come back to it every
time a character tries to become someone slightly else. It is the reason the
book is shaped the way it is: the tables that make a person are read once,
and the table about changing is read constantly, so it lives at the edge of
the book where the flag sticks out furthest.

Throw d66. First die: what they must give up (1 time, 2 money, 3 a person, 4
a belief, 5 standing, 6 the story they told about themselves). Second die:
how long it takes, 1 being a night and 6 being the rest of the campaign.

That is thirty-six answers, and I could have written them all out as
sentences, and I have deliberately not. Two numbers and a scale is enough.
Some tables should be short.

How to use a flagged book at a table
------------------------------------


Tape the flags before session one. All twelve, even the ones you think you
will not need.

RUN WITH THE BOOK SHUT. This is the test of whether the system is doing its
job. If you can find where did they come from with the book closed, by feel,
in under two seconds, the book is working. If you have to open it and check
the index, I have failed and you should tell me which flag was in the wrong
place.

MOVE A FLAG WHEN IT IS WRONG FOR YOUR TABLE. They are tape. My flag 7 is the
money because my games are about the money. If your table's recurring stall
is who do they owe, move flag 6 to the outside edge and push 7 in. The
numbers do not matter. The reach does.

WHEN YOU ADD A TABLE OF YOUR OWN, GIVE IT A FLAG NUMBER AND A RETURN. Both.
A house table with no return is exactly as stranding as one of mine would
be, and you will not notice until somebody is sitting there at eleven at
night with a die in their hand and nowhere to go.

AND ONCE A SEASON, PULL A FLAG AT RANDOM AND READ WHAT IS UNDER IT.

I want to be careful about this, because I have just spent a whole book
telling you that finding the table is the hard part, and somebody is going
to take that and stop there.

A flag on a table that no longer says what you play is worse than no flag at
all. No flag and you go looking. A flag and you reach, and trust, and read
out a rule your table stopped using in March, and nobody catches it because
the tab was right there and confident. Findability without correctness is a
faster route to the wrong answer.

So: one flag a season, pulled blind, read cold, against how you actually
play. If what is under it has gone stale, fix it or take the flag off. A tab
you cannot trust is a liability wearing the costume of a solution, and I
would rather you found that out from me than at eleven at night.

Errata, and one other thing
---------------------------


CORRECTIONS IN THIS PRINTING. Flag 7's band table was published with a
reused number and is corrected in place on the page, with the original left
visible, because a book about finding the page should not hide the page it
got wrong. Flags 6, 8 and 11 were listed in the index of the first draft and
had no tables under them -- a book whose whole argument is no dead ends
shipped four of them, and I would rather you heard that from me. They are
written now. The trade table on flag 4 was named and not printed; also
written now. And the header lines on flags 5, 7 and 9 still described the
old routing after the body had been corrected -- a tab pointing at a stale
table, in the book that tells you to check for exactly that, three pages
earlier. I would like to say I caught it. The checker caught it.

If you find a fifth, the address is at the front and I will put it in the
next printing with your name on it.

TWO MORE, AND THE SECOND IS WORSE THAN THE FIRST. Someone read a printing of
this book and reported that the tables in the middle of it were, and I am
quoting, very hard to follow. She was right. Four of the big lookups were
set six columns wide, and six columns of written-out sentences do not
survive a page -- every column squeezes to a sliver and the grid stops being
a grid. They are set in halves now, units 1 to 3 and then 4 to 6, which is a
worse-looking table and a readable one. I would rather be read.

Worse: fixing the width made me look properly at flag 6, and flag 6 was
broken. It ran who they owe and what kind of debt together in one six-column
table, which meant a second die of 1 handed you a second person instead of a
debt. Six of the thirty-six results were not results. The who is its own
short list now and the debt grid is complete -- six entries in that grid are
new in this printing, on rows 1 through 6 of the second half.

So the reader who could not follow the middle of the book was not having
trouble with my table. She was having trouble with my table's mistake, which
had been sitting there in plain sight wearing a layout problem as a
disguise. My own tooling had been warning me about the width for some time.
I read the warning and decided it was about the plain-text edition. It was
not.

THE OTHER THING. I am told there is an arrangement behind these four books
and that my colleagues have written variously about it -- one at length, one
declining, one apparently at some emotional cost.

I have read theirs. Mine is three sentences.

A machine threw dice at a list and got reckless, refuses all direction,
untidy, and competitive, and marked the last one load-bearing. It is not
