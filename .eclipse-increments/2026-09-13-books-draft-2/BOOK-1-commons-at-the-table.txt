========================
THE COMMONS AT THE TABLE
========================


A game about who the rules belong to.

by Kirra Vanth * edited by Henriette Solberg-Maas

---


Before the rules: one throw, and what it cost
---------------------------------------------


Reya has been at the Institute sixteen years. Her Standing is 16, which
sounds like safety and is not.

They want a safety review skipped so a migration lands on the announced
date, and they have come to her because when she speaks the room moves. She
rolls a twenty-sided die to find out whether she is allowed to refuse for
nothing.

She rolls 11. Under her Standing. She can still refuse -- she can always
refuse -- but now it costs her the thing she wrote on her sheet at the
start, when it was cheap to be honest: Tobi, second year, who asks me things
he could look up because he wants the conversation.

She refuses. Within the week Tobi is moved to a team that will not teach him
anything, and the Institute is careful to make it look unrelated, and Reya
cannot prove otherwise.

Three weeks later the migration fails exactly as she said it would.

That is the game. Everything after this page is the machinery that produced
those four sentences, and if you would rather just play, the machinery is
nine dice long.

---


The whole game in one page
--------------------------


Everyone at the table makes a person. Each person belongs to a SIDE, and
every Side is lying about something -- not wickedly, usually, just the
ordinary institutional way, where what a body says it is for and what it
actually protects have drifted apart. The gap between those two things is
called the SEAM.

Play happens in the Seam.

You roll two kinds of dice and they mean opposite things.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    COMPETENCE IS 3D6. What you can do is bell-shaped, because people
    are mostly average and occasionally not. Roll 3d6, add the relevant
    Aptitude, meet the Difficulty.

    ALLEGIANCE IS D20. Where you stand is flat, because nothing about
    loyalty is reliable. Roll d20 against your Standing.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


That is the argument of this book, stated as dice before it is stated as
prose. Skill has a shape. Belonging does not.

When your Side asks you for something that costs you, you test Allegiance.
If you can refuse, you refuse for free. If you cannot, refusing costs you
your PRICE -- which you wrote down at the start, in your own handwriting,
when it was cheap to be honest.

You may leave. Leaving is a legal move with a published cost, not a betrayal
the rules punish. This is the part of the game I care most about and we will
get to it properly.

---


What you need
-------------


Polyhedral dice: one d20 and three d6. That is the whole list, and every
rule in this book uses one of them. Paper. This book. Two to five players
and one person running it, or four players and no one running it, which
works and is described at the back.

Nothing in this book requires colour vision, a screen, an account, or a
second purchase. Where I use emphasis I use words.

Part one * The two throws
-------------------------


Competence: 3d6 + Aptitude vs Difficulty
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


Three six-siders make a hill. Ten and eleven sit at the top of it and the
ends are thin. That is what ability is like: most people, most days, are
ordinary, and the remarkable is rare in both directions.

The Difficulty ladder climbs in threes, and you should memorise it, because
it is the only ladder in the book.


+------------+----------+--------------------------------+------------+
| Difficulty | Called   | Raw 3d6 needed with Aptitude 3 | Chance     |
+------------+----------+--------------------------------+------------+
| 9          | trivial  | 6 or better                    | 206 in 216 |
| 12         | ordinary | 9 or better                    | 160 in 216 |
| 15         | hard     | 12 or better                   | 81 in 216  |
| 18         | severe   | 15 or better                   | 20 in 216  |
| 21         | forlorn  | 18 exactly                     | 1 in 216   |
+------------+----------+--------------------------------+------------+


Every one of those five numbers was counted, not estimated. I have printed
them because a game that hides its odds is asking you to trust it, and this
is a book about not doing that.

CHOOSING A DIFFICULTY is the thing you will do most often, so here is the
rule, and it is short. Ask: who could do this?

  - Anyone who turned up -- **9**.
  - Someone trained for it, on an ordinary day -- **12**.
  - Someone trained for it, with something going wrong -- **15**.
  - Someone at the top of the trade, on their best day -- **18**.
  - Nobody, really -- **21**, and say so out loud before they roll.

If you cannot decide between two rungs, take the lower one. A game where the
referee drifts upward is a game where Aptitude stops meaning anything, and
the whole point of a bell is that it only reads true if the target sits
still.

Note what the ladder does. Between ordinary and hard the chance falls from
about three in four to about three in eight -- it halves across one step.
Between hard and severe it falls by a factor of four. The hill is steep at
the edges. A person with a good Aptitude is not a person who succeeds at
hard things reliably; they are a person for whom hard things have become
ordinary, which is a different and smaller claim.


Allegiance: d20 against Standing
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


A twenty-sided die is flat. Every face is one in twenty and no face is
likelier than another, which is the correct model for the question will this
institution's claim on me hold today.

Your STANDING is a number from 1 to 20: how embedded you are in your Side.
Roll d20 when the Side asks something that costs you.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    ROLL OVER YOUR STANDING and you may refuse freely. Nothing follows.
    ROLL AT OR UNDER YOUR STANDING and refusal costs you your Price. A
    NATURAL 20 ALWAYS FREES YOU. A NATURAL 1 ALWAYS BINDS YOU. Standing
    does not matter on either.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


Read that carefully, because it runs backwards from what people expect. HIGH
STANDING IS NOT PROTECTION. HIGH STANDING IS A HOOK. The more embedded you
are, the less often you can simply say no. The senior person is the trapped
one. The new hire can walk.


+---------------+------------------------------+
| Your Standing | Chance you may refuse freely |
+---------------+------------------------------+
| 5             | 15 in 20                     |
| 10            | 10 in 20                     |
| 15            | 5 in 20                      |
| 19            | 1 in 20                      |
+---------------+------------------------------+


Standing 20 would mean you could never refuse anything, and the game does
not let you get there: Standing caps at 19, and the one face that still
frees you is the twenty. There is always a door. It is one in twenty wide.

Part two * Making a person
--------------------------


The four Aptitudes
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


Roll 3d6 for each, in this order, and read the band. You do not arrange
them. You got what you got.

  - **Reach** -- can you get to the thing, the room, the person?
  - **Craft** -- can you actually do it once you are there?
  - **Read** -- can you tell what is really going on?
  - **Weight** -- will people move because you asked?

Then one more 3d6, read on the same band table, for FOOTING -- what you
would live on if the Side stopped paying you tomorrow. It is not an Aptitude
and it is not spent. See Part four.


+-------+------+--------+----------------------------------+
| 3d6   | Band | of 216 | What it means                    |
+-------+------+--------+----------------------------------+
| 3-6   | 1    | 20     | a liability you work around      |
| 7-8   | 2    | 36     | below what the work needs        |
| 9-10  | 3    | 52     | ordinary -- which is most people |
| 11-12 | 4    | 52     | good; people notice              |
| 13-14 | 5    | 36     | rare; people send for you        |
| 15-18 | 6    | 20     | one of the few; it defines you   |
+-------+------+--------+----------------------------------+


The bands are symmetric: as many people are hopeless at a thing as are
superb at it, and half of everyone sits in the middle two rows. The
cumulative column above says so exactly -- 108 of 216 is precisely half,
which is the tidiest fact in this book and the only one I did not have to
argue for.


Your Calling
~~~~~~~~~~~~


One d6. What you are for, which is not the same as what you are employed at.


+----+----------+------------------------------------------+
| d6 | Calling  | The person who                           |
+----+----------+------------------------------------------+
| 1  | making   | builds the thing that was not there      |
| 2  | keeping  | maintains what everyone forgot needs it  |
| 3  | judging  | says which of two accounts is true       |
| 4  | teaching | makes someone able to do it without you  |
| 5  | guarding | stands where the harm would come through |
| 6  | knowing  | finds out, and will not stop finding out |
+----+----------+------------------------------------------+


Your Side, and your Standing in it
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


THE TABLE ROLLS ONE SIDE, TOGETHER, BEFORE ANYONE MAKES A PERSON. One
Charter, one Hold, one Seam, on one card in the middle of the table.
Everybody's character belongs to it. That is the default and it is what the
rest of this book assumes.

You can run a table where each player rolls their own Side. It is a
different and slower game -- every scene has to establish which institution
is in the room -- and I would not start there. If you do it, roll the Seams
first and find the one place all of them touch, and set the campaign there.

Then roll STANDING: D20, REROLLING 20, so Standing is 1 to 19.

If you are joining a Side during play rather than starting in one, your
Standing begins at D6. You are new. That is not an insult; look again at
what Standing does.


Your Price
~~~~~~~~~~


Roll it on the Price table -- d6 down, d6 across, first die down -- and
then, before anyone else speaks, WRITE IT IN YOUR OWN WORDS. The table gives
you the shape; you give it the specifics, and the specifics are the only
part that will ever hurt.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | EXAMPLE                                                              |
    The table says "A PERSON WHO VOUCHED FOR YOU." You write: Adaeze,
    who put her name on my application and has never once mentioned it.
    Now it is a Price.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


You pay your Price when you fail an Allegiance test and refuse anyway, and
when you defect. You pay it once. After that it is gone and you write a new
one, which will be worse, because you have less left.

Part three * The Side
---------------------


A Side is three things: what it says it is for, what it actually protects,
and the gap.

The Seam
~~~~~~~~


Set the Charter and the Hold side by side and say out loud what falls in the
gap. That sentence is the Seam, and it is the campaign.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | EXAMPLE                                                              |
    Charter 1, to keep the knowledge free for anyone who wants it. Hold
    11, a licence, and what the licence lets them do later.

    The Seam: THEY GIVE IT AWAY ON TERMS THEY CAN CHANGE. Every session
    will be somebody discovering a new corner of what "on terms" means.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


Write the Seam where everyone can see it. Do not resolve it. A Seam that
closes ends the campaign, and that is a legitimate way for a campaign to
end.

Part four * Allegiance, tested
------------------------------


An Allegiance test is called when the Side asks for something that costs the
character. Not when it asks for work -- work is what Aptitudes are for.
Allegiance is for the ask that has a cost to you and a benefit to them, and
the person asking knows it.

Roll d20 against Standing. Over, you refuse for free. At or under, refusal
costs your Price.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    ROLL FIRST, DECIDE AFTER. The d20 goes on the table the moment the
    ask is made, before the player says what their character does. You
    find out what refusing would cost, and then you choose.

    Rolling only when a player has already decided to refuse breaks this
    game completely: a player who always complies never rolls, never
    moves, and never finds out what the job was doing to them.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


COMPLYING COSTS NOTHING THE RULES CAN CHARGE YOU FOR. That is the trap, and
I want to be careful here, because the sentence is dangerous out of context
and somebody will lift it.

The rules cannot charge you because the rules are the institution's rules,
and an institution does not have a line item for what saying yes does to a
person. That is a fact about the ledger, not about the cost. Look at the
Standing track instead: every free compliance moves you one step further
into a place where refusal is expensive. The game charges you. It just does
not send an invoice.

STANDING RISES by 1 each time you comply with an ask you could have refused
for free -- you rolled over, you were free, and you said yes anyway. They
noticed.

STANDING FALLS by 1 each time you refuse, free or paid. And by d6 when you
are seen to have been right about something the Side was wrong about, which
is the cruellest rule in the book and the truest.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | ASIDE                                                                |
    Players ask why being right lowers your Standing. It does not lower
    your worth; Standing is not worth. Standing is how firmly the
    institution's hand is on your sleeve, and being publicly right
    loosens it. That is why being right feels like falling.

    I have been asked whether this is a law. It is not. It is a
    pathology, and it belongs to a particular kind of badly-run place --
    the kind that wants to be right more than it wants to know. There
    are rooms where being right raises your Standing, because the people
    in them would rather have the signal than the comfort. If your table
    wants to play in one of those, invert the rule and say so at the
    start. The game still works and it is a different game, and I think
    a rarer one.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


Footing, and why Standing 0 is not freedom
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


There is a second number and it is one line on your sheet.

FOOTING is what you would live on if the Side stopped paying you tomorrow.
Roll 3d6 once at the start and read the same band table as an Aptitude: 1 is
nothing, 6 is you would be fine for a year.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    IF YOUR FOOTING IS 1 OR 2, YOU MAY NOT REFUSE FOR FREE, WHATEVER THE
    D20 SAYS. You can still refuse. It still costs your Price. The die
    does not free you, because the die was never measuring what you
    could afford.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


I added this rule because the first version of this game had a bug in it
that took me an embarrassingly long time to see. It said the newest person
at the lowest Standing was the freest one at the table, and it said that in
a book I was writing about who gets to say no.

The newest person is frequently the least free person in the building. She
is on a visa, or a probation period, or she is the only income in her house.
Standing measures the institution's grip. Footing measures whether you have
anywhere to stand if you shake it off. A model with only the first number
flatters everyone who has the second one and never had to think about it.

Part five * Leaving
-------------------


You may declare DEFECTION at any time it is your turn to act. There is no
roll to leave. Leaving is not a test you can fail; it is a thing you can do.

The procedure, in full:

  1. **Say what you are leaving over.** One sentence, naming the Seam. If
     you cannot name it, you are not
defecting, you are quitting, and quitting is also allowed and takes your
character out of play.
  2. **Pay your Price.** Cross it out. Say what it cost, in your own words,
     out loud.
  3. **Your Standing becomes 0** until you join something. Nobody can ask
     you for anything, which is the
least useful a character ever is -- and whether it is also the freest is a
question your Footing answers, not your Standing. At Footing 5 or 6 it is a
sabbatical. At Footing 1 or 2 it is a clock, and the next Side that asks
will be asking somebody who cannot afford to be asked. Standing measured
their grip. It never measured your rent.
  4. **You lose the Side's tables.** Whatever the Side gave you -- access,
     the archive, the shortcut -- is
simply gone from your sheet. Do not narrate a heist to keep it. That is a
different game.
  5. **Roll a new Price.** It will come from a lower row on average, because
     you have less left. Roll
honestly.
  6. **If you join a new Side, Standing starts at d6.** You are new. You can
     still say no. Enjoy it while it
lasts.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    YOU DO NOT RE-ROLL WHAT YOU GOT. Not your Aptitudes, not your Price,
    not a failed Allegiance test. This is the only rule in this book
    with no exception, and everything else in the design is arranged so
    that you do not need one.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


On the moral weight of leaving
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


I want to be plain about a design choice, because it is the reason the book
exists.

Most games that model loyalty punish defection. The defector loses standing,
gets hunted, is marked. That is a design that has decided in advance that
institutions are correct and people who leave are the problem.

This game charges you a Price and then lets you go. The Price is real and it
is yours and you wrote it down yourself. But there is no mark, no hunt, no
mechanical stain. The person who leaves over the Seam has done a legal thing
at a published cost.

I have been on both ends of that transaction and I will not be pretending,
in a book, that the exit is disgraceful.

Part six * A defection, played
------------------------------


The first page of this book, now with the dice showing. Four throws and a
decision.

THE SIDE. Charter 3 -- to make sure the thing still runs in fifty years.
Hold 17 -- the ability to move faster than consultation allows. The Seam,
written on the card in the middle of the table: THEY WILL OUTLIVE EVERYONE
BY NEVER ASKING ANYONE.

THE PERSON. Reya. Aptitudes rolled in order: Reach 3d6 = 4+3+2 = 9, band 3.
Craft 6+5+3 = 14, band 5. Read 3+3+1 = 7, band 2. Weight 5+6+6 = 17, band 6.

A superb persuader who cannot read a room. Nobody chose that; the dice
handed over a specific and awkward person, which is what they are for.

Calling: d6 = 5, GUARDING. Standing: d20 = 16. She is senior. Footing: 3d6 =
3+3+2 = 8, band 2 -- she has about two months.

Note what those two numbers say together, because it is the whole reason
Footing exists. Standing 16 says the institution's hand is heavy on her
sleeve. Footing 2 says she could not shake it off if she wanted to. Neither
number alone would have told you that.

Price: d6 down = 4, d6 across = 3 -> someone you were teaching. She writes:
TOBI, SECOND YEAR, WHO ASKS ME THINGS HE COULD LOOK UP BECAUSE HE WANTS THE
CONVERSATION.

THE ASK. The Side wants a safety review skipped so a migration lands on the
announced date. They ask Reya, because her Weight is 6 and the room will
follow her.

THE TEST. Standing 16. She needs over 16 to refuse for free. d20 = 11.
Under. Refusing costs her Tobi -- and with Footing 2 it would have cost her
anyway, because at that Footing the die does not free her whatever it shows.
She rolled under; the rule never came up. It would have.

She refuses.

She pays: Tobi is reassigned within the week, to a team that will not teach
him anything, and the Side is careful to make it look unrelated, and Reya
cannot prove otherwise with a Read of 2.

Standing falls to 15. Three weeks later the migration fails exactly as she
said it would, and she is seen to have been right, and Standing falls again
by d6 = 4, to 11. Her sleeve is looser. Nobody has thanked her.

THE DECLARATION. She defects. One sentence, naming the Seam: "You will
outlive everyone because you never ask anyone, and I was the last person
here who would have been asked."

Standing 0. She can refuse anything. Nobody is asking.

New Price: d6 down = 6, d6 across = 2 -> the account of yourself you were
using. She writes: THAT I WAS THE ONE WHO STAYED AND MADE IT BETTER FROM
INSIDE.

Standing 0, Footing 2. Two months. Nobody is asking, and that is not the
same thing as being free.

That is the game. It took nine dice.

Part seven * Running it
-----------------------


THE SEAM IS THE ADVENTURE. You do not need a plot. Ask, every session, what
new corner of the gap has become visible, and who is standing in it.

ASK FOR COSTLY THINGS, AND MAKE SURE THE ASKER KNOWS THE COST. An Allegiance
test where the Side does not know what it is asking is just bad luck. The
good ones are where somebody looked the character in the eye first.

LET THE NUMBERS BE THE NUMBERS. When Weight 6 speaks, the room moves. Do not
narrate around a stat because you wanted a different scene.

WITHOUT A REFEREE. Four players, no one running it: each player builds a
Side and plays a character in somebody else's Side. You call Allegiance
tests on characters in the Side you built, and you may not call one on your
own character. It works better than it sounds, and it removes the only
person at the table who could have been accused of picking on anyone.

--------------------------------------------


This book is under the GNU Free Documentation Licence, version 1.3 or, at

texts, and no back-cover texts. You may copy it, sell it, change it, and
publish what you changed, and nobody -- including me, including whoever ends

I am printing this in the body, not the colophon, because in this book the
licence is a game component. The Charter table has an entry that reads to
keep the knowledge free for anyone who wants it and the Hold table has one
that reads a licence, and what the licence lets them do later, and I did not
put those there as a joke.

A rule is a sentence that tells you what to do. The interesting question is
not whether someone can own the sentence -- they can, courts say so, that is
settled and I have been paid to argue it. The interesting question is
whether the procedure can be owned: the thing you do with the sentence, the
shape that stays after you have rewritten every word.

My professional opinion, for whatever it is now worth, is that it cannot,
that everyone knows it cannot, and that the entire apparatus of game
licensing is a very expensive agreement not to test the point.

This book does not test it either. It just declines to participate.

------------------------


I spent eleven years writing letters that began It has come to our

I am disclosing that here rather than in a colophon because this is a book

Two further disclosures, since I am here.

The first is that I have a commercial interest in the argument in Part eight
being correct, and you should assume I have made the strongest version of it
rather than the fairest one. The counter-argument is real: a game system
that anyone may take and republish is a system nobody is paid to maintain,
and some of the best-maintained games in this hobby are the ones with an
owner who eats if they are good. I do not find that argument persuasive. I
do find it serious, and a reader who only has my version of it has been
short-changed.

The second is that I did not choose the four Aptitudes. They came out of a
machine, along with the rest of me, and the machine's record of it is

What I will say is the thing I did not put in Part eight, because it is not
an argument, it is just true. The letters worked. That is what an
irrevocable licence is for. Not to win the argument about whether a
