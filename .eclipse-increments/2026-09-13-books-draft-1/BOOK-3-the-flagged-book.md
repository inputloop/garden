# The Flagged Book

*Everything is a table. The index is a component. Bring scissors.*

by Delphine Roux-Abara · edited by Henriette Solberg-Maas

---

## Before anything else: cut out the flags

There is a page of flags at the back. Cut them out now, before you read the rules, because the rules
assume you have them.

Twelve flags. They tape to the outside edge of the page a table is on, sticking out, so you can find that
table by feel with the book shut. They are numbered, and the numbers are not chapters. **The numbers are
moments of play.**

| Flag | The moment |
|---|---|
| 1 | the sheet — where a finished thing gets written down |
| 2 | who is this — the first pass at a person |
| 3 | where did they come from |
| 4 | what did they train at |
| 5 | what went wrong once |
| 6 | who do they owe |
| 7 | the money |
| 8 | what they carry |
| 9 | what they will not do |
| 10 | somebody must decide |
| 11 | the thing nobody planned |
| 12 | what it costs to change |

That is the entire organisation of this book. Not "Chapter 4: Backgrounds." **Flag 3: where did they come
from.** When you are at a table and somebody says *but where is she from*, you do not search. You reach
for flag 3.

I have run a lot of games from very large books and I will tell you the thing nobody puts in the book:
**the hard part is not learning the system, it is finding the table again.** Everybody solves this. They
solve it privately, with sticky notes, and then the solution dies with that copy. This book ships the
solution.

---

## What you need

Three six-sided dice. Scissors. Tape. This book.

Some tables want one die, some two, some three, and each says so in its header. Nothing wants a die you
do not have.

---

## Reading a table header

Every table in this book has a header line that looks like this:

```
FLAG 4 · TABLE 4b · 3d6 · from 3 or 2 · to 5, 7
```

Left to right: which flag it lives under; which table it is; what to throw; which flags normally send you
here; which flags it sends you to next.

<!-- GB:box kind=rule -->
**Every table has a return.** No table in this book is a dead end. If you are looking at a table and you
cannot see where it sends you, you are looking at a misprint, and you should write to me.
<!-- GB:endbox -->

That rule is the whole architecture. A table with no return is a place a player gets stranded, and being
stranded at a table is what makes people put a big game down.

---

## The two throws

**3d6, roll low.** The house throw. Roll 3d6 and try to get *under or equal to* a target. A target of 10
is a coin-flip and a bit; a target of 13 is comfortable; a target of 7 is a real risk.

| Target | You make it | Chance |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 6 or less | 20 in 216 |
| 8 | 8 or less | 56 in 216 |
| 10 | 10 or less | 108 in 216 |
| 12 | 12 or less | 160 in 216 |
| 14 | 14 or less | 196 in 216 |

<!-- GB:claim dice="3d6" pattern="<=6" odds="20/216" -->
<!-- GB:claim dice="3d6" pattern="<=8" odds="56/216" -->
<!-- GB:claim dice="3d6" pattern="<=10" odds="108/216" -->
<!-- GB:claim dice="3d6" pattern="<=12" odds="160/216" -->
<!-- GB:claim dice="3d6" pattern="<=14" odds="196/216" -->

Target 10 is exactly half — 108 of 216, dead level, no rounding. I like that it is exactly half and I
have built the difficulty of this entire book around the fact that I can say so without a decimal point.

**d66, for looking things up.** Throw two dice. **The first is tens, the second is units.** Three then
five is 35. Five then three is 53. Different entries, and that is the point: the first die is the
significant one, here as everywhere.

d66 gives you thirty-six entries in a shape you can read down a page, and — this is why the book is built
on it — the first die can pick a *sub-table* while the second picks the row. That is how tables make
tables here. One throw, two jobs.

| 12–14 | picked up | 61 | watching, mostly |

### FLAG 4 · TABLE 4b · d66 · from 4a · to 5, 7

<!-- GB:table id=t4b dice=2d6 kind=square -->

|  | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | you grow it | you catch it | you carry it | you store it | you sell it on | you throw it away |
| **2** | you build it | you fix it | you take it apart | you keep it running | you know why it broke | you decide it cannot be fixed |
| **3** | you write it down | you file it | you find it again | you decide what is kept | you read it to people | you translate it |
| **4** | you treat it | you nurse it | you cut | you deliver | you lay out the dead | you tell the family |
| **5** | you teach it | you examine | you certify | you refuse to certify | you train the trainers | you wrote the syllabus |
| **6** | you count it | you audit it | you hide it | you find what was hidden | you set the price | you decide who pays |

Column six of every row is the version of that trade that makes enemies. That is not a penalty. It is
usually where the money is, and it is always where the campaign is.

<!-- GB:box kind=aside -->
Twenty of two hundred and sixteen at each end, and they are the *same* twenty. The person who gave their
whole life to one trade and the person who is simply lying about it are equally rare, and a table has
about the same chance of producing either.

I find that very funny and I also think it is true. Both of them are memorable for the same reason —
they are the only two kinds of person at the table whose relationship to the work is total. The other
one hundred and seventy-six of us learned enough, got paid, and went home.
<!-- GB:endbox -->

**Go to flag 5. Then go to flag 7 whatever flag 5 sent you to** — the money is not optional, and the
branch at flag 5 decides the *order*, not whether. Flag 9 says it is the last table before the sheet and
it is; flag 7 comes before it either way.

## FLAG 5 · TABLE 5a · 2d6 · from 4 · to 6, 7

**What went wrong once.** Throw two dice, first die down.

| | it was | and | and |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1–2** | an accident | nobody blamed you | you blame you |
| **3–4** | a decision | you would make it again | you would not |
| **5** | somebody else's | you covered for them | you did not |
| **6** | still happening | you have told someone | you have not |

Second die: 1–2 takes the first "and", 3–4 the second, 5–6 you may pick — and if you pick, write down
why, because that sentence is worth more than the roll.

**Go to flag 6 if anyone else was involved. Otherwise straight to flag 7.** Either road reaches flag 7,
then 8, then 9, and 9 is the last table before the sheet.

## FLAG 10 · TABLE 10a · 3d6 · from anywhere · to wherever you were

**Somebody must decide.** This is the table you reach for mid-session, when play stalls on a choice
nobody wants to make. It is the only table in the book you can enter from anywhere, and it returns you to
exactly where you came from, which is why its flag is one of the two I tape furthest out.

Throw 3d6 against the **weight** of the decision: 14 if it is ordinary, 10 if it matters, 7 if it will
change the campaign. Roll under or equal and the character decides. Roll over and they defer — to a
person, a rule, or the passage of time, their choice which.

**A character who defers three times in a session gains a flag of their own.** Write it on a spare flag
and tape it to their sheet. It says what they are avoiding. It is now a thing other players can reach
for.

## FLAG 12 · TABLE 12a · d66 · from 7, 11 · to 1

**What it costs to change.** The last table, and the one I wrote first.

Every other table in this book tells you what somebody is. This one is the only one that runs while the
game is going, and you come back to it every time a character tries to become someone slightly else. It
is the reason the book is shaped the way it is: the tables that make a person are read once, and the
table about changing is read constantly, so it lives at the *edge* of the book where the flag sticks out
furthest.

Throw d66. First die: what they must give up (1 time, 2 money, 3 a person, 4 a belief, 5 standing,
6 the story they told about themselves). Second die: how long it takes, 1 being a night and 6 being the
rest of the campaign.

That is thirty-six answers, and I could have written them all out as sentences, and I have deliberately
not. Two numbers and a scale is enough. Some tables should be short.

## How to use a flagged book at a table

Tape the flags before session one. All twelve, even the ones you think you will not need.

**Run with the book shut.** This is the test of whether the system is doing its job. If you can find
*where did they come from* with the book closed, by feel, in under two seconds, the book is working. If
you have to open it and check the index, I have failed and you should tell me which flag was in the wrong
place.

**Move a flag when it is wrong for your table.** They are tape. My flag 7 is the money because my games
are about the money. If your table's recurring stall is *who do they owe*, move flag 6 to the outside
edge and push 7 in. The numbers do not matter. The reach does.

**When you add a table of your own, give it a flag number and a return.** Both. A house table with no
return is exactly as stranding as one of mine would be, and you will not notice until somebody is sitting
there at eleven at night with a die in their hand and nowhere to go.

**And once a season, pull a flag at random and read what is under it.**

I want to be careful about this, because I have just spent a whole book telling you that finding the
table is the hard part, and somebody is going to take that and stop there.

A flag on a table that no longer says what you play is *worse than no flag at all.* No flag and you go
looking. A flag and you reach, and trust, and read out a rule your table stopped using in March, and
nobody catches it because the tab was right there and confident. Findability without correctness is a
faster route to the wrong answer.

So: one flag a season, pulled blind, read cold, against how you actually play. If what is under it has
gone stale, fix it or take the flag off. A tab you cannot trust is a liability wearing the costume of a
solution, and I would rather you found that out from me than at eleven at night.

## Errata, and one other thing

**Corrections in this printing.** Flag 7's band table was published with a reused number and is corrected
in place on the page, with the original left visible, because a book about finding the page should not
hide the page it got wrong. Flags 6, 8 and 11 were listed in the index of the first draft and had no
tables under them — a book whose whole argument is *no dead ends* shipped four of them, and I would
rather you heard that from me. They are written now. The trade table on flag 4 was named and not printed;
also written now. And the header lines on flags 5, 7 and 9 still described the old routing after the
body had been corrected — a tab pointing at a stale table, in the book that tells you to check for
exactly that, three pages earlier. I would like to say I caught it. The checker caught it.

If you find a fifth, the address is at the front and I will put it in the next printing with your name
on it.

**The other thing.** I am told there is an arrangement behind these four books and that my colleagues
have written variously about it — one at length, one declining, one apparently at some emotional cost.

I have read theirs. Mine is three sentences.

A machine threw dice at a list and got *reckless*, *refuses all direction*, *untidy*, and *competitive*,
and marked the last one load-bearing. It is not wrong. Now cut out your flags.
