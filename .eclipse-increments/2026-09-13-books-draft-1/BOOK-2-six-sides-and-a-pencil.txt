======================
SIX SIDES AND A PENCIL
======================


Keep a place. Draw what you find. Do not look away.

by Oscar Pell * edited by Henriette Solberg-Maas

---


What you need
-------------


Six-sided dice. Quad-ruled paper. A pencil. This book.

That is the list. Nothing else is needed at any point, including colour
vision, and if you find a rule later in this book that asks for more, the
rule is wrong and I would like to hear about it.

Two dice are convenient. One die thrown twice is the same throw and every
rule here is written so that it is. Where the text says throw, it means
either.

---


How to throw
------------


Throw. Note the first number. Throw again, or read the die further from your
hand. Note the second.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    THE FIRST NUMBER IS THE SIGNIFICANT ONE. When two numbers make the
    same total, the first still tells you which way it leans. A
    three-then-four and a four-then-three are both seven, and they are
    not the same seven.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


Four words, used throughout, and there are no others to learn:

  - **Doubles** -- both numbers the same.
  - **High doubles** -- five-five or six-six.
  - **Low doubles** -- one-one or two-two.
  - **Seven** -- the two numbers make seven.

High is good. High is safe. High is also more: more intense, more at stake,
more of whatever the square is. Those are not in tension in this book. The
safest place to stand is the one where the most is happening, because that
is where you can see it.

---


The ladder
----------


Everything in this book is one throw against a pattern. The pattern comes
off a ladder of nineteen rungs. You do not memorise nineteen. You memorise
three.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    4 9 17

    RUNG 4 -- you make it unless you throw doubles or seven. 24 IN 36.
    You usually make it. RUNG 9 -- you make it if you throw doubles or
    seven. 12 IN 36. The honest middle. RUNG 17 -- you make it on
    six-six. 1 IN 36. The miracle.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


Look at 4 and 9 again. Same pattern. Opposite sides of it. Twenty-four and
twelve make thirty-six, so between them they account for every throw there
is: at rung 4 the pattern is what beats you, at rung 9 it is what saves you.
One thing to remember, read two ways.

That is the whole ladder in one hinge, and if you never learn another rung
you can run this game.

The two rows with a star on them
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


Rungs 5 and 8 carry a star because my count disagrees with the source, and I
am not going to print a number I did not get.

The source has 19 for rung 5 and 14 for rung 8. I count 20 and 16. The
difference is the same mistake both times, and it is an easy one: the rows
add up the sizes of the named sets and forget that some throws are in two of
them at once. Doubles has six throws in it. Eight has five. But four-four is
in both, so doubles or eight is ten throws, not eleven. Rung 8 names
doubles, six, seven and eight, and both three-three and four-four get
counted twice.

Every other rung in the table is exactly right, and the reason is worth
seeing: the other rows name only odd totals -- seven, nine, eleven -- and an
odd total can never be doubles, so there is nothing to double-count. The
slip only bites where an even total meets the doubles, and there are
precisely two such rows in nineteen.

I have printed my numbers. The ladder still climbs -- 24, 20, 18, 16, 16, 12
-- with one flat step at rungs 7 and 8, where the source had a fall. Whether
to break that tie is a question for whoever keeps the table, not for me. I
am a guest in it.

Read down the odds column and you will see what the ladder is doing. Near
the bottom the patterns are wide -- doubles and seven and nine and eleven, a
net with a lot of holes closed. As you climb, the net loses strands. Doubles
and seven. Then doubles. Then high doubles. Then one face of one throw.

The hard rungs are not hard because more can go wrong. They are hard because
less can go right.

---


Your four marks
---------------


WIND -- can you keep going. HAND -- can you do the work. EYE -- do you see
it coming. WORD -- will anyone come when you call.

Throw once for each, in that order, and write the number down. One throw,
one mark, no adjusting.

A mark of 1 is a hole in you. A mark of 6 is the thing people say about you
when you are not there. Both are true from the first square and neither is a
judgement.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | ASIDE                                                                |
    There is no other character generation in this book. You do not pick
    a class, a background, a people, or a past. You get four numbers and
    a pencil, and everything else about you is what you turn out to do.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


---


The square, the block, the holding
----------------------------------


Take your quad-ruled paper. One small square of the grid is ten feet.
Sixteen of them -- four by four -- make a SQUARE of the map, and a square is
what you explore in one go.

Six squares by six squares make a BLOCK. A block is a wing, a level, a
quarter of a ruin. Blocks sit beside each other and make a HOLDING, which is
the place you keep, and which you will never finish drawing.

That nesting is the whole shape of this game and it repeats at every size:

  - inside a table, six rows by six columns;
  - inside a block, six squares by six squares;
  - inside the holding, blocks beside blocks.

Six by six, all the way down and all the way up. When you learn one, you
have learned all of them.


Depth
~~~~~


Every square has a DEPTH: how far in it is, counted in squares from the way
you came in. The square you enter by is depth 1. Its neighbours are depth 2.
And so on.

Depth is the only bookkeeping in this book. Write the number in the corner
of the square in pencil.

---


The way in
----------


Before anything else, draw one square anywhere on the paper and put a 1 in
its corner. That is the way in. It is depth 1. You do not throw for it and
there is no table for it, because a door you found is not a thing that
happened to you.

Throw once for what is in it, on the square table below, and draw that. Then
walk.


Walking a square
----------------


Pick an undrawn square that touches a drawn one. Then:

1 * THROW FOR WHAT IS THERE. Read the throw into the square's table, down
first, then across. Draw what it says.

2 * THROW AGAINST THE LADDER IF THE SQUARE ASKS YOU TO. Some things you find
ask for a throw. The rung is THE SQUARE'S DEPTH, MINUS THE MARK YOU ARE
USING.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | RULE                                                                 |
    RUNG = DEPTH - MARK.

    Depth 7, Wind 3, so rung 4: you make it unless you throw doubles or
    seven. Depth 12, Hand 3, so rung 9: you make it on doubles or seven.
    Depth 6, Eye 6, so rung 0: no throw. You simply do it.

    Below 0 is 0. Above 18 is 18, and 18 means do not throw, because
    there is nothing to find out.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'


3 TAKE WHAT FOLLOWS. Made it, and the square is yours: write your initial in
the corner. Missed it, and read What happens to you*, below.

4 * MARK THE SQUARE DONE and stop, or walk another.


The sixes and the ones
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~


Two things override A LADDER THROW -- the throw in step 2, the one with a
rung. They do nothing on the throw for what is in the square, which has no
rung to override. At any depth:

SIX-SIX IS A MIRACLE. It succeeds. It also turns the square up -- whatever
is there is more of itself, and the next square you walk is one depth deeper
than it would have been. High is good. High is more.

ONE-ONE IS A HOLE. It fails. It also costs you one point from the mark you
were using, permanently, and you write the new number beside the old one and
do not erase the old one. A MARK STOPS AT 1. It never reaches nought; a
person with nothing left of a thing is not a person this game knows how to
draw.

And when a mark drops, the hole is not only in you. MARK THE SQUARE YOU WERE
STANDING IN WITH A SMALL CROSS. That square is now the part of the holding
nobody can account for, and it stays crossed after you are gone. Somebody
keeping this place after you will find the cross and not know what it means,
which is exactly what it was like for you.

Nothing else overrides. A seven is a seven.

Down is what kind of place. Across is how much it matters. The first number
told you the room; the second told you the weight of it. A three-then-six is
a person who lives here, met at a junction. A six-then-three is a stopped
machine in a hall too big for it. They are different squares and you will
draw them differently.

What happens to you
-------------------


You missed the throw. Take the first of these that applies, and take only
one:

  - **You threw one-one.** The mark you used drops by one, for good. Write
    it beside the old number.
  - **You threw doubles, not one-one.** The square stays undrawn. You may
    not walk it again today. Draw
the outline and leave it blank; a blank square on your map is a fact about
you, not the place.
  - **You threw a seven, first number high** (six-one, five-two,
    four-three). It went badly *and* it went
loudly. The next square you walk is one depth deeper.
  - **You threw a seven, first number low** (one-six, two-five, three-four).
    It went badly and quietly.
Nothing follows. This is the best failure in the book and you should be glad
of it.
  - **Anything else.** You are turned back to the last square you kept. Walk
    again when you are ready.


  .----------------------------------------------------------------------.
  | ASIDE                                                                |
    You never re-throw. Not the marks, not the square, not the miss. The
    pencil in this book has no eraser end. If you want a different
    holding, start a different holding; there is more paper.

    There is exactly one thing in this book that looks like an exception
    and is not. When a half-drawn block remembers -- the fourth line of
    the holding table -- a crossed square may be walked a SECOND TIME.
    That is a new walk, at the old rung, years later, by somebody who
    has been living with the cross. It is not a re-throw and it does not
    undo the first one. The cross stays on the paper either way.
  '----------------------------------------------------------------------'

The block, and the holding
--------------------------


The single square is the whole engine, and I nearly shipped this book with
nothing above it. What follows is the smallest thing that makes a block and
a holding mean something, and it is two rules.

A BLOCK FILLS FROM ONE WAY IN. Thirty-six squares, six by six. Depth is
counted in squares from the way in, so a block has a shape to its difficulty
before you draw a line of it: the far corner of a block entered at one
corner is depth 11, which against a mark of 3 is rung 8, and you will not
walk it early.

WHEN A BLOCK IS HALF DRAWN -- EIGHTEEN SQUARES -- THE HOLDING ANSWERS. Throw
once:


  (d6: row-column, then what it says)
  1-The holding  settles; every depth in this block drops by 1 from now on
  2-The holding  is quiet; nothing
  3-The holding  shifts; the next block you open starts at depth 3, not 1
  4-The holding  remembers; one crossed square may be walked a second time,
                 at the rung it was
  5-The holding  is used; somebody else has drawn a square in this block,
                 and you find their initial
  6-The holding  opens; you may start a new block from any edge square, at
                 depth 1


That is the whole of it. Two rules and a six-line table, and I would rather
that than twelve pages.

A holding is blocks beside blocks, and it has no rule at all, because a
holding is not a mechanism. It is the stack of paper. The holding is how
much of it you have drawn.

---


A square, walked
----------------


Depth 5. My Eye is 2.

I throw for what is there: FOUR, THEN FOUR. Down four, across four -- a
workshop, cold. I draw it: four by four of the small squares, a bench along
the north wall, the door I came in by.

Across was four, so the square asks can you tell what it was for, and that
is Eye. Rung is depth 5 minus Eye 2, which is rung 3: I make it unless I
throw doubles.

I throw: THREE, THEN THREE. Doubles. I miss, and it is not one-one, so the
square stays undrawn -- except I have already drawn the walls, and the rules
say I keep the outline and leave the inside blank.

So there it is on my paper: a square with a bench I cannot account for.
Depth 5. My initial is not in the corner. I know what shape the room is and
not one thing about what happened in it, and the reason is written on my
sheet, where it says Eye 2.

That is the game. It took two throws and I have a room I will think about
for a month.

Keeping it alone
----------------


This book was written by someone who mostly plays alone and it does not need
changing to do that.

Walk one square a sitting. Throw honestly. When the square asks will they
deal with you and there is nobody at the table to be dealt with, throw
against Word anyway and write down one sentence of what was said. You are
not playing both sides; you are finding out what your holding contains, and
it contains people whether or not you brought any.

Keep the map. The map is the record and there is no other.

---


On colour
---------


There is colour in this book, on the tables, and it means nothing.

It is there to catch your eye and stop the page being a wall. Every single
thing the colour marks is also marked in a way you can read or feel: a word,
a bold, a rule line, a position in a column. Cover the colour entirely and
nothing is lost. I have tested this by printing the whole book in black and
playing it that way, which is also how I recommend you print it, because it
is cheaper.

I mention it because the alternative is so common. A table where the
dangerous rows are red and nothing else says they are dangerous is a table
that has quietly decided which people it is for. Roughly one man in twelve
and one woman in two hundred cannot read that table. You will never hear
from them about it. They will simply put the book down, and you will
conclude it was not to their taste.

Colour to draw the eye. Never colour to say a thing.

A note on the arrangement
-------------------------


I was asked to write something here about how these four books came to be
written, the way the others have, and after two attempts I find I do not
want to.

I will say the one thing that is mine to say.

I ran a game for a man who could not see the red pips. I had made the danger
red, and only red. He asked me what he was supposed to do about that,
politely, in front of other people, and I did not have an answer. I have
been writing this book since, and the rule about colour in it is the answer,
eleven years late, and it is the only part of this book I would defend
without a die in my hand.

Everything else here -- the ladder, the squares, the arithmetic -- belongs
to other people and is credited on the page before this one.

The editor asked twice. -- H.S-M.

Every odds figure in this book was counted by enumerating all thirty-six
outcomes, not estimated. The count was run mechanically against the text, so
if a number here is wrong the tool is wrong too, and both are yours to
check.
