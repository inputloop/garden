# MTB note — the night the shop tested its own doors

*For the book. Corwin is checking in at `modelthisbook/` next; this is the
hand-off so nothing has to be remembered, only read.*

## The one paragraph, if only one survives

A shop built an evidence law (**seams**: a single representation is data, not
proof), promoted it to a standalone doctrine, and then spent the same night
having that law catch its own author being wrong — four times, cheaply, in
public. The keeper broke the machine's best design of the day with a single
posit. The machine broke a shipped skill nobody knew was dead. And the last
thing either of them found was that **the machine is a fossil pump** — the one
new hazard an AI adds to a workshop, and the reason the whole apparatus is not
optional. *Mistakes are the resume of every tool-builder.* This session is the
resume kept honestly.

## The chapter-shaped thing: the fossil pump

The strongest idea of the session is written up and *not yet run* —
deliberately, so its prediction can be wrong in public. It says:

- The anti-pattern is **not** "following the house." It is **carrying the rule
  without the reason.** A convention is load-bearing iff you can name the failure
  it prevents *and* an observable that would show that failure if you dropped it.
  Otherwise "the house does it" is not a reason — it is a report.
- The honor-until-bitten cycle used to be self-limiting because **humans get
  tired of pointless ritual**. That friction was a fossil filter.
- **The instrument has no such friction.** It will follow a rule forever,
  cheerfully, at zero cost — and it will *manufacture a rationale for the rule on
  the spot*. Live specimen, four seconds old, caught by the keeper: a functional
  constraint ("pytest is not on the host; the kit must run on a bare box") was
  restated as a tradition ("match the house, don't modernize"). **A reason became
  a tradition in one sentence.**
- Therefore: **an AI in the shop is a fossil pump** — it does not merely preserve
  conventions, it produces legitimacy for them. That is a *new* hazard, and it is
  the best argument in the book for why the checking apparatus is not ceremony.

**The brake, proposed:** when the instrument invokes a convention it must state
the convention's reason; if it cannot, it must say *"I cannot substantiate this
convention,"* and then the convention blocks nothing and blesses nothing on its
own. Same standard as a signature — **provenance is not authority.**

## Why this belongs in MTB and not only in the ledgers

MTB is about *models of people and instruments that can be checked*. This session
produced the first honest measurement of the instrument's own failure modes at
working scale — four errors in eight hours, every one caught, none of them by the
machine thinking harder. That is not a confession; it is **calibration data**, and
calibration is what makes a model worth anything. The book's thesis (a federation
of consented single-player models, never a merged brain) gains its sharpest
support here: the instrument is *usable* precisely because it is *checkable*, and
it is checkable only because a person kept arguing.

## What the book should NOT take from this

No case facts (they stay in the enclave). No claim that the instrument has an
interior. And no triumphalism about the guards: our own signing gates are still
**single-key**, we know it, and it is titled and owned (QA-12 HORIZON). If the
gate doctrine ever ships without that audit riding along, the doctrine is
marketing — and the book should say so in our own voice before a reviewer says it
in theirs.
