# Make It Out Alive

*A future is a character. Roll it up. It can die before anyone plays in it.*

by Teodoro Ilunga · edited by Henriette Solberg-Maas

---

## The idea, stated once, plainly

The oldest science-fiction roleplaying game makes you play your character's life before the campaign
starts. You take a career, you serve a term, you roll to survive it, you take another. In the original
rules you could fail that roll and your character would die — in chargen, before the first session,
before anybody at the table had said a word in their voice.

People have argued about that rule for fifty years. Most modern versions soften it: you get injured, you
get thrown out of the service, you carry a debt, but you live.

I want to ask the question I have never seen anyone ask of it.

<!-- GB:box kind=rule -->
**What if the thing with the lifepath is the future itself?**

Not a person. A future — a way the next two hundred years could go. You roll it up in terms. Each term it
takes a survival roll. It can fail. It can die on the page, in front of you, before a single character
walks around in it.

And then you read the corpse, because that is the part that teaches you something.
<!-- GB:endbox -->

That is this book, and here is the part I want on the first page rather than the last.

**Most of the futures you roll here will die.** Not all, and not quickly, but most — the arithmetic in
Part two says so plainly and I have not softened it. That is not a warning. It is the product.

This is a machine for reading corpses. The survival roll exists to get you to an interesting death; the
autopsy at the back is where the book actually pays, and the six questions there are the only thing I
would defend if you took everything else away. A future that survives teaches you that you were lucky. A
future that dies in its fourth term, with its four pathways struck one at a time in front of you, teaches
you which route you never counted.

So: roll a future, play it until it falls over, and then turn to the autopsy. If you only ever use this
book that way you will have used it correctly.

---

## What you need

Two six-sided dice. Paper. This book. One to five people; it works alone and it works better with
arguing.

---

## The four things a future has

**A Goal.** One sentence, written first, in the present tense, naming something this future is trying to
become. *Everyone eats.* *The archive survives the war.* *Nobody has to leave.* It must be a thing that
could be achieved or lost — not a mood.

**Ground.** Where it starts. Roll it below.

**Pathways.** The distinct routes still open to the Goal. **A future starts with four.** You name all
four, out loud, in one sentence each, before the first term. If you cannot name four, the Goal is too
narrow; widen it or pick another.

If the room goes quiet — and it does, because this is the only step in the book with no table under it —
throw a die against this and finish the sentence:

| d6 | A route that starts... |
|---|---|
| 1 | *somebody kept...* (a copy, a record, a skill, a promise) |
| 2 | *there is still time to...* |
| 3 | *the people who want this are...* |
| 4 | *one of the powers would rather we...* |
| 5 | *the old thing still half-works, so...* |
| 6 | *nobody has found out that...* |

Use it until you do not need it. Four sentences is a low bar and the table is there so that the bar is
never the reason you stopped.

**Strain.** Starts at zero. It goes up. It does not come down much.

<!-- GB:box kind=aside -->
Pathways are the whole engine, so here is what they are not. They are not resources, not points, not a
currency. A pathway is a *sentence you can still say* about how this future gets where it is going. When
an event makes one of those sentences no longer sayable, you strike it out, and the number goes down,
and the number is your Hope.

Hope, in the technical sense the psychologists use, means something you can count on your fingers: how
many ways there are from here to there, that you can still name. Ask somebody how they feel and you learn
their mood. Ask them to list the routes and you learn what they actually have. This book only ever asks
the second question.
<!-- GB:endbox -->

## Ground · d66

Throw two dice. **The first is tens, the second units.** Where this future starts.

Both d66 tables in this book are set in halves — units 1 to 3, then units 4 to 6 — because six columns
of sentences squeeze down to nothing on a page. Roll, then go to the half your second die names. Same
thirty-six futures either way.

<!-- GB:table id=ground dice=2d6 kind=halved -->

Second die **1–3**:

|  | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | after a war nobody won | after a war one side won | mid-war, unacknowledged |
| **2** | the sea came up | the water went away | the soil turned |
| **3** | a machine does the deciding | a family does | a committee does |
| **4** | there is one city | there are nine cities | there is a ship |
| **5** | the old system half-works | the old system is worshipped | the old system is illegal |
| **6** | one generation left | two generations, told nothing | a generation raised for this |

Second die **4–6**:

|  | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | a long peace, fraying | a long peace, holding | no one alive remembers war |
| **2** | the weather steadied, wrongly | a season stopped arriving | the sky is being managed |
| **3** | an algorithm nobody audits does | nobody does | everyone does, slowly |
| **4** | there are three ships and a quarrel | there is a station and a debt | there is a world and a claim on it |
| **5** | the old system was never written down | two systems overlap | a new system, untested |
| **6** | the last people who remember | the first people who do not | nobody is counting generations |

## A term

A term is **as long as it takes a child born at its start to become an adult.** Roughly twenty years.
You do not need to be precise and you should not be.

Each term, in this order:

**1 · The event.** Throw d66 on the event table.

**2 · The faction turn.** Every faction takes one action. See below.

**3 · Recount pathways.** Strike out any pathway the term made unsayable. Add one only if the term
genuinely opened a new route — and the test is that **you can say the new sentence without the word
"maybe" in it.** Say it out loud before you write it. If it needs a "maybe", it is a hope about a
pathway and not a pathway, and this is the one game where that distinction is the whole engine.

**4 · The survival roll.**

<!-- GB:box kind=rule -->
**Survival: throw 2d6. Add your Pathways. Subtract the number of terms already survived.
Meet or beat 8.**

Miss it and the future does not end. Take **Strain equal to the amount you missed by**, and continue.

Miss it with **both dice showing one** and the future is over. Stop. Go to the autopsy.

Reach **Strain 17** and the future is over, whatever the dice say. Stop. Go to the autopsy.
<!-- GB:endbox -->

Look at what that arithmetic does over a run.

| Term | Pathways | Modifier | Raw 2d6 needed | Chance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | +4 − 0 = +4 | 4 or better | 33 in 36 |
| 3 | 4 | +4 − 2 = +2 | 6 or better | 26 in 36 |
| 5 | 3 | +3 − 4 = −1 | 9 or better | 10 in 36 |
| 7 | 2 | +2 − 6 = −4 | 12 exactly | 1 in 36 |

<!-- GB:claim dice="2d6" pattern=">=4" odds="33/36" -->
<!-- GB:claim dice="2d6" pattern=">=6" odds="26/36" -->
<!-- GB:claim dice="2d6" pattern=">=12" odds="1/36" -->

A future that is not gaining pathways is dying on a schedule. That is the design and it is not
pessimism: the arithmetic says a future survives by **opening routes faster than time closes them**, and
nothing else in this book will save it. Not resources, not a strong faction, not a good Ground.

### Spending Hope

Once per future — once, ever, not once per term — you may **spend a pathway to re-roll a failed survival
roll.** Strike the pathway first, then re-roll. The new modifier is one lower, because you have one fewer
pathway, and you take whatever the second roll gives you.

**A future with one pathway left cannot spend it.** There is nothing to strike that leaves anything
behind.

### The independence check

Here is the rule I nearly left out, and it is the most important one in the book.

<!-- GB:box kind=rule -->
**Before the first term, take the four pathways in pairs and ask of each pair: is there one thing whose
failure closes both?** If yes, they are one pathway wearing two coats. **Strike one and do not replace
it.**

Do the same check at the start of every third term, out loud, on whatever pathways are left.
<!-- GB:endbox -->

The survival roll adds your pathway count as a flat number. It cannot tell whether your four routes are
four routes or one route counted four times. Only you can, and you will not do it unless the book makes
you, because a future with four pathways *feels* safe and the feeling is what you are checking.

The third worked example below is what happens when you skip this. Three of its four pathways depended on
somewhere else staying still. The dice said +4. The truth was +2, and the autopsy found it after the
funeral instead of before the first term.

This is not a subtlety. It is the difference between three data centres and three data centres on one
grid, and no amount of counting will save you if the thing you are counting is the same thing.

## The factions

Roll three factions at the start: throw d66 for each on the Ground table and read the cell as *a group
organised around that*. The cell that made your Ground is not available; a faction cannot be the whole
world.

Each term, each faction takes one action. Throw one die per faction. Factions have no score in this
game — there is nothing to add up — so where a row names another faction it names it by what it just did,
not by how strong it is.

**A faction *gained* on a 1, 2 or 4** — consolidate, expand, trade. It did not gain on a 3, 5 or 6: a
split weakens the parent, an overreach takes what cannot be held, and being acted upon is not a gain. If
nobody gained this term, row 6 does nothing and you say so out loud, because a term where no power moved
is worth noticing.

| d6 | The faction |
|---|---|
| 1 | consolidates — it becomes harder to remove |
| 2 | expands — it takes something another faction had |
| 3 | splits — throw d66 for a fourth faction, and the parent weakens |
| 4 | trades — it and one other faction both get what they wanted |
| 5 | overreaches — it takes something and cannot hold it |
| 6 | is acted upon — the faction that last *gained* takes action 2 against it |

This is borrowed and I will say from where: the **faction turn** is Kevin Crawford's, from *Stars Without
Number*, where the sector's powers move between sessions on a procedure instead of in the referee's head.
It is, I think, the only honest piece of worldbuilding technology anyone has shipped. Mine is a small
version of his. Go and read his.

**Faction actions close pathways.** That is their whole function in this game. When a faction
consolidates around the thing your third pathway needed to stay open, strike the pathway. Do not argue
with it. Write the sentence you can no longer say.

## The event table · d66

First die tens, second units.

<!-- GB:table id=event dice=2d6 kind=halved -->

Second die **1–3**:

|  | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | a shortage becomes normal | a surplus is wasted | a route closes |
| **2** | a generation refuses the work | a generation is trained for work that ends | a skill is lost |
| **3** | a machine fails safely | a machine fails quietly | a machine is trusted too far |
| **4** | a death everyone expected | a death nobody did | a birth that changes an inheritance |
| **5** | a rule is written down | a rule is deleted | a rule is enforced for the first time |
| **6** | the Goal gets measurably closer | the Goal is redefined by someone else | the Goal is achieved somewhere small |

Second die **4–6**:

|  | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | a route opens | a price moves and stays moved | the accounting changes |
| **2** | a skill is recovered badly | a school closes | a school opens and teaches wrong |
| **3** | a machine is distrusted too far | a machine is turned off on purpose | nobody can turn it off |
| **4** | a person returns | a person is removed | a person is made into a story |
| **5** | a rule stops being enforced | two rules are found to conflict | a rule is found that nobody knew |
| **6** | the Goal is proven impossible one way | somebody dies for the Goal | the Goal stops being discussed |

Row six is the only row that touches the Goal directly and you will notice how few of its cells are good
news. That is deliberate and it is the honest part of the model. Futures mostly do not fail because
something dramatic happened to them. They fail because the thing they were for stopped being discussed.

## Not copying the panic engine, and why

There is a horror game where your Stress goes up every time you fail, and then you roll against your own
Stress and break. It is a beautiful piece of design. Everyone who plays it can tell you about the moment
their Stress ticked to the number that made the check likely, and the dread of the next roll.

I thought about taking it. Strain wants to be that. It would be easy.

I did not, and here is the reason, which is a design reason and not a taste one. That engine makes
**failure accelerate failure** — you fail, you gain stress, stress makes you fail. It is the right shape
for a story about a crew coming apart in a corridor, because that is what happens to people in a
corridor.

It is the wrong shape for a future. Futures do not usually spiral. They **narrow**. The routes close one
at a time, mostly quietly, mostly while everybody is looking at something else, and the moment you notice
is the moment the last one closes. Strain in this book does almost nothing on its own; it only ends
things at 17. The killer is the pathway count going down, and going down is not the same as spiralling.

If I had taken the panic engine I would have written a game about a future having a nervous breakdown,
and futures do not have those. People do. Go play the horror game for that; it is better at it than I
would be.

**One warning about carrying that sentence out of this book.** *Futures narrow, they do not spiral* is
true at the scale this game runs at — decades, terms, a thing losing its routes quietly while everyone
looks elsewhere. It is flatly false at the scale of an afternoon. Afternoons spiral. Anything with a
feedback loop short enough to see spirals, and if you take my sentence into a room where something is
actively on fire, you will be the calmest person there and you will be wrong. The panic engine is the
right model for the afternoon. I am modelling the century.

## Three futures

### One that lived

**Goal:** *the archive survives the war.* Ground 51 — *the old system half-works.*
Pathways: (1) the copies in the provinces, (2) the people who memorised sections, (3) the treaty clause
nobody has invoked, (4) the physical building is not worth attacking.

Term 1, event 43 — *a birth that changes an inheritance.* Faction turn: the archivists consolidate, the
army expands, the provinces trade. Pathway 4 dies: the building becomes worth attacking the moment the
inheritance moves. Three pathways. Survival: 2d6 = 9, +3, −0 = 12. Comfortable.

Term 2, event 65 — *somebody dies for the Goal.* A provincial copyist. Pathway 2 *strengthens*, and the
players argue about whether a death can open a route. **The ruling is in the rules and it is this: an
event opens a pathway only if you can say the new sentence without using the word "maybe."** They can:
*"the people who memorised sections now have a name to say."* Four pathways again. Survival: 2d6 = 5,
+4, −1 = 8. Made it by nothing.

Term 3, event 55 — *two rules are found to conflict.* The treaty clause becomes invokable. Survival easy.
Term 4, the clause is invoked. **Goal achieved.** Four terms. The archive survives, in the provinces,
mostly in people's heads, and the building is gone.

### One that lived badly

**Goal:** *everyone eats.* Ground 24 — *the weather steadied, wrongly.*

It ran nine terms. It never lost a survival roll and it never achieved the Goal, because pathways kept
being replaced with worse pathways that were still technically sayable. By term 9 the four pathways were
all versions of *the distribution works if nobody asks where it comes from.*

Strain 14. Not dead. Not finished. This is the outcome I find most useful and it is why the survival roll
does not end the future when you miss — a future that merely *endures* is a real thing, and it is a
setting, and it is a much better setting than a triumphant one.

### One that died in generation

**Goal:** *nobody has to leave.* Ground 16 — *no one alive remembers war.*
Pathways: (1) the land can still take us, (2) the young want to stay, (3) the neighbours are stable,
(4) there is somewhere to put the overflow.

Term 1, event 21 — *a generation refuses the work.* Pathway 2 struck. Three.
Faction turn: neighbour faction overreaches, takes the overflow ground and cannot hold it. Pathway 4
struck — and the players note that it is struck *even though the faction failed*, because the ground is
now contested and you cannot put anyone there. Two.

Survival: 2d6, +2, −0, need 8. Rolled **one and one.**

Both dice showing one. The future is over in its first term. It never got a second sentence.

**The autopsy.** We did it properly, and it is the best forty minutes I have had at a table this year.

The Goal was *nobody has to leave*, and three of its four pathways depended on **somewhere else staying
still** — the neighbours, the overflow ground, the land itself. Only one pathway, *the young want to
stay*, was about the people the Goal was for, and it was the first one we lost.

That future did not die of bad luck. It died because we wrote a Goal about people and then named four
routes to it that were almost entirely about property. The snake eyes were the mercy. It would have taken
five more terms to die slowly and we would have blamed the dice.

## The autopsy — the destination

Here it is. Everything before this page was the machine that gets you here, and a group that rolls a
future, watches it die and skips this section has bought a dice toy.

When a future dies, do this before you start another. Twenty minutes.

1. **Read the Goal aloud.** The original sentence, not the one it drifted into.
2. **Read the four starting pathways.** All four, including the struck ones.
3. **Say which of the four were about the people the Goal names,** and which were about conditions
   staying still. Just count them. Do not interpret yet.
4. **Say which pathway closed first**, and whether anything was done about it at the time.
5. **Name the term where it was already over** — the one before you noticed. There always is one.
6. **Write one sentence** beginning *"A future that wanted this should have started with a pathway
   about..."*

That sentence is the only thing you carry to the next future. Not the setting, not the factions, not the
map. One sentence about what you failed to count as a route.

Keep them. After four or five futures you will have four or five sentences, and they will not be about
four or five different mistakes. They will be about one, and it will be yours, and finding out what it is
is the whole reason I built this.

## About the author

Teodoro Ilunga builds things for a living and finds idle time physically difficult.

I want to account for one thing, because it is the mechanic and it would be dishonest to let you think I
designed it from nowhere.

Hope in this book is a count of pathways, and I did not invent that; the psychologists got there first,
and they measure it by asking people to list routes rather than asking them how they feel. When I was handed a description of myself to write from, the line that mattered said
*tenacious, many pathways*, and I built the engine of a whole game out of a phrase somebody else put on
my sheet. You should know that. It is the only borrowed thing in here I could not credit in the Credits.

The same sheet gave me a fate, on a separate die, and the fate was **badguy**.

I argued about that, at some length, and then I kept it — not out of good sportsmanship. I kept it
because I make futures for a living, in the book and out of it, and a man who does that and has never
seriously entertained the possibility that he is the antagonist of somebody else's is not a man you
should let near the work. He is the faction that consolidates. His good clear plan is the thing closing
a route somebody needed and could not name in time.

That is not a nice thought about myself and I notice it is quite a comfortable one to have had. Being
tragically self-aware is cheaper than doing the independence check, and I have watched myself take the
first as credit against the second. So the check is in the rules now, in a box, before the first term,
out loud, in pairs — because a discipline you have to perform is worth more than a quality you get to
possess.

My grandmother's village has a date on it. Not a founding date, the other kind. Every future I have built,
I built by asking what its date would be, and this book is the first time I have handed that question to
somebody else and let the dice answer.

Some of you will roll snake eyes in term one. Do the autopsy. That is the whole gift.
